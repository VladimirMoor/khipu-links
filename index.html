    A register of the links between surviving Inka khipus

    Every link we found between khipus is a copy of one account

    We ran arithmetic detectors over about 700 digitized khipus and compared every hit with a baseline that keeps the khipus' structure. What survives is a set of copies and excerpts. Some keep only the totals of an account, some only its details, some split one account over two khipus or join two accounts on one. We found no summary khipu built from other surviving khipus.

        Find numbers

        One number finds every pendant (and, optionally, group total) with that value. Two or more numbers find runs of consecutive pendants or of consecutive group totals, in either direction. Values come from the catalogue layer (pendants' own values).

        Compare two khipus

        Aligns the pendant sequences (forward and reversed), lists identical groups, and finds the group totals of one khipu among the cords of the other, with the count expected by chance from values offset by ±1…±5.

            What is a khipu?

            A khipu (also spelled quipu, from the Quechua word for “knot”) is a set of knotted cords that people in the Andes used to keep records. Most surviving khipus come from the Inka empire, which ruled the Andes from Ecuador to Chile in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, until the Spanish conquest of 1532. Some are older, from the Wari state (about AD 600–1000). Khipus were made of cotton or camelid fibre, often dyed, and could hold from a handful to more than a thousand cords.

            Spanish chroniclers describe specialists, the khipukamayuq, who kept khipus of the census, of tribute and of the contents of storehouses, and who “read” them aloud to officials. Some villages in Peru still kept khipus as community records in the twentieth century.

            How numbers were knotted

            Many khipus record numbers in a decimal system that has been understood since 1912 (L. L. Locke). Each hanging cord carries one number. Position along the cord gives the place value: knots nearest the main cord are the highest place (thousands, hundreds), knots farthest from it are the units. An empty position means zero.

            Where do they come from?

            Roughly a thousand khipus survive in museums around the world. Most come from burials on the dry Pacific coast of Peru and northern Chile, where the desert preserved the fibre. Many were dug up in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, often by grave robbers (huaqueros), and sold to collectors and museums in Lima, Berlin, New York and elsewhere. As a result, the place recorded for a khipu is often only what a dealer said. About 700 khipus have been recorded cord by cord and are openly available as data; this atlas uses them.

            What we know, and what we don't

            We can read the numbers on most khipus, and many khipus add up: one cord often holds the sum of a group of others. Khipus from an Inka storehouse at Inkawasi record deliveries of produce with a fixed deduction taken from each. What the numbers counted — people, llamas, bags of maize — is usually unknown, because the labels were kept by colours, cord order and the memory of the reader, not in writing. Whether some khipus also recorded words or stories is still debated. Only a handful of khipus have been matched to Spanish written records, such as khipus from the Santa valley and a colonial census (Medrano 2021).

            What this project does

            Most studies look inside one khipu at a time. We looked for numbers shared between different khipus: one khipu that copies another, or one that holds the totals of another. Such links show how accountants handled several records of the same business — who kept the details and who kept the totals. With a computer we compared every khipu in the open data with every other one.

            Because the corpus is large, some coincidences are bound to happen. So every link is tested against chance: we run the same search with the numbers slightly shifted, which keeps everything else the same, and count how many matches appear by accident. A link is reported only if it stands far above that level. Weaker patterns are labelled “suggestive”, and the searches that found nothing are listed under Negative results.

            How to use the atlas

            • Overview — the main results on one page.
            • Map — where the khipus were found; red arcs join places with linked khipus. Switch to 3D relief to see the coast and the Andes.
            • Links — a network of the khipus that are linked to each other.
            • Catalogue — every khipu, with its museum, find-place, a 3D model and its cord values. Type a number such as AS143 or UR212 in the search box at the top.
            • Findings — one page per result, with the evidence and the scripts that reproduce it.
            • Tools — search for a sequence of numbers across all khipus, or compare two khipus.
            • Timeline, Statistics, On the object (what only a visit to the museum can settle), Sources.

            Words used on this site

            Primary cord
            the main cord from which the others hang.
            Pendant
            a cord hanging from the primary cord; it usually carries one number.
            Subsidiary
            a cord tied to another cord rather than to the primary cord.
            Top cord
            a cord rising above the primary cord, often holding a sum.
            Group
            pendants set close together, separated from the next group by a space; groups often have the same structure.
            Single, long and figure-eight knots
            single knots count tens, hundreds and higher places; a long knot (2–9 turns) or a figure-eight knot (one) gives the units.
            AS, UR, KH, MM…
            catalogue numbers. AS numbers were given by Marcia and Robert Ascher, UR numbers by Gary Urton, KH numbers by the Khipu Field Guide; one khipu can have several. Museum numbers such as VA 42508 belong to the museums.
            OKR, KFG
            the Open Khipu Repository and the Khipu Field Guide, the two open databases of khipu recordings used here.
            Copy, extract
            a khipu that repeats the values of another, completely or in part.
            Baseline
            how many matches the same search finds by chance; a result counts only if it is far above it.

            To read further

            Gary Urton, Inka History in Knots (2017); Marcia and Robert Ascher, Code of the Quipu (1981; the Databook is online at Cornell); Frank Salomon, The Cord Keepers (2004), on khipus kept in a Peruvian village. Our full results are in the preprint.

            About

            This atlas collects the results of a corpus-wide search for arithmetic links between khipus in the open data of the Open Khipu Repository and the Khipu Field Guide, with two khipus we transcribed from record-sheet scans. Every detector was first run on relations already published, and every result was compared with structure-preserving baselines.

            Data and code

            Scripts, the full report and the study log are in the repository github.com/VladimirMoor/khipu-links; the paper is the preprint (PDF, version 3). Cord data come from the OKR (MIT licence) and the KFG; please cite them when you use the values.

            How this was made

            The analysis and this site were produced with the help of an AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic), directed and checked by the author. Transcriptions made from scans were checked against the recorders' own totals.
